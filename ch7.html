Cultural Transformations

AP World History, Chapter 7. How Christianity became a global religion, how Islam and the traditions of China and India renewed themselves, and how modern science was born, about 1450 to 1750.

Work through it in order: read a section's summary, try its margin questions before opening the answers, and mark it as reviewed. Then use the timeline, comparison charts, primary sources, and short answer practice, drill the key terms as flashcards, and finish with the quiz.

The big picture

Alongside new empires and new trade, the early modern centuries remade the world's cultural map. Christianity, still mostly a European faith in 1500, was splitting apart at home in the Protestant Reformation even as it followed Spanish, Portuguese, French, and Russian expansion to become a genuinely global religion, rooted above all in the Americas and the Philippines. Islam kept spreading across Africa and Asia and produced its own reform movements, while Confucian China and Hindu India generated new ideas from within. In Europe, the Scientific Revolution created a new way of knowing, based on reason and evidence, that grew into the Enlightenment and eventually spread worldwide.

The traffic did not run one way. Native Americans, enslaved Africans, Chinese scholars, and Mughal emperors decided what to accept, what to reject, and how to blend new ideas with their own. European science drew on Islamic learning and on a flood of knowledge from the wider world.

Four themes to remember

  • Christianity spread most where European power was overwhelming and no literate world religion existed.
  • Almost everywhere, converts blended new faiths with older beliefs: this is syncretism.
  • Reform movements challenged established authority in Europe, Arabia, China, and India alike.
  • The Scientific Revolution replaced authority with evidence and reason, and the Enlightenment applied that to society.

The Protestant Reformation

In 1500 Christianity was still mostly a European religion, split between Roman Catholics in the west and Eastern Orthodox Christians in the east, and it was on the defensive against Islam. The Ottomans had taken Constantinople in 1453 and would besiege Vienna in 1529 and again in 1683. Then in 1517 a German priest, Martin Luther, issued his Ninety-Five Theses, calling for debate about church abuses: the luxury of the popes, corrupt clergy, and the sale of indulgences, which were said to cancel the penalties for sin.

Complaints about the Church were nothing new. What made Luther revolutionary was his theology. Salvation came through faith alone, not through good works or the Church's sacraments, and religious authority came from the Bible alone, read by each believer's own conscience, not from the pope and clergy. His ideas spread because they fit other grievances. Kings and princes saw a chance to break with the pope and take church lands and taxes. Urban middle classes liked the idea that every occupation had equal religious worth. Peasants used the new ideas to attack the whole social order, as in the German peasant revolts of the 1520s. The new printing press carried Luther's pamphlets and his German New Testament everywhere.

Protestantism then split into many churches, including Lutheran, Calvinist, Anglican, Quaker, and Anabaptist, none loyal to Rome. (John Calvin added the doctrine of predestination: God has already chosen who will be saved.) Religious difference now divided people within and between states. French Catholics fought the Protestant Huguenots from 1562 to 1598, and the Thirty Years' War (1618 to 1648) killed an estimated 15 to 30 percent of Germany's population. The Peace of Westphalia (1648) ended that war by making each state sovereign, with the right to control religion inside its own borders. Women gained little. In Protestant areas the veneration of Mary and female saints ended and convents closed, and although Bible reading raised women's literacy, they had few chances to use it outside the home.

Key points

  • Faith alone and the Bible alone: Luther's theology challenged the authority of the pope and clergy.
  • It spread because it served political, economic, and social interests, and because of printing.
  • Religious wars followed, and the Peace of Westphalia made religion each sovereign state's business.

CausationWhat were the long-term and short-term causes of the Protestant Reformation?

Long-term: long-standing anger at church corruption, papal luxury, and indulgences, plus rulers' disputes with the pope and a growing urban middle class. Short-term: Luther's Ninety-Five Theses in 1517 and his theology of faith alone and the Bible alone, spread quickly by the printing press.

Continuity and changeHow did the Reformation change European politics and society?

It ended the religious unity of Western Christendom, added religious conflict to existing class and political divisions, caused wars like the Thirty Years' War, and strengthened state sovereignty through the Peace of Westphalia. Women's roles changed little.

The Catholic Reformation and its legacy

The Protestant breakaway, along with reformers inside the Church, produced a Catholic Reformation, also called the Counter-Reformation. At the Council of Trent (1545 to 1563), the Church restated the beliefs Protestants had rejected: the authority of the pope, priestly celibacy, the veneration of saints and relics, and the importance of tradition and good works. It also went after the abuses that had fueled the revolt by improving the education of priests and their supervision by bishops. Dissent was suppressed through censorship, fines, exile, and sometimes the burning of heretics.

New religious orders carried the renewal forward, above all the Society of Jesus, or Jesuits, a disciplined brotherhood of priests devoted to reviving the Church at home and spreading it abroad. The Christianity that went global after 1500 was more divided than before, but also re-energized.

The Reformation also had an unintended intellectual effect. By defying the pope and surviving, it encouraged skepticism toward authority and tradition. It promoted religious individualism, since people now read scripture and sought salvation for themselves. Later, some Europeans turned that habit of independent thinking against religion itself, which opened room for new directions in European thought, including science.

Key points

  • Council of Trent: restate Catholic doctrine and fix the abuses Protestants attacked.
  • The Jesuits led Catholic renewal and overseas missions.
  • The Reformation's challenge to authority helped open the way for the Scientific Revolution.

Christianity outward bound

Religion drove European expansion and benefited from it. The Spanish and Portuguese saw their overseas ventures as a continuation of the crusades that had only recently pushed Muslims out of Iberia. When Vasco da Gama's crew reached India in 1498, they said they had come looking for "Christians and spices," and saw no contradiction in that. Settlers brought their faith with them. The Puritans of New England planted a strict Protestant Christianity that stressed education, moral purity, and personal conversion, but they showed little interest in converting Native peoples and instead pushed them off their land.

Most active missionary work was Catholic, organized by orders such as the Dominicans, Franciscans, and Jesuits. Portuguese missionaries led the way in Africa and Asia, while Spanish and French missionaries were most active in the Americas. Russian Orthodox priests and monks followed Russia's expansion across Siberia, where trappers often gave their first sable furs to a church or monastery.

Missionaries had their greatest success in Spanish America and the Philippines, which shared two conditions. First, an overwhelming European presence of conquest, settlement, forced labor, and disease made it seem that the old gods had lost. Second, neither region had a literate world religion. Peoples rooted in Confucian, Buddhist, Hindu, or Islamic traditions resisted Christianity far more than peoples whose religions were local and oral. Christianity made almost no headway in the Islamic world.

Key points

  • Empire spread Christianity, and Christianity justified empire.
  • Catholic missionary orders did most of the converting.
  • Success depended on European power and the absence of a literate world religion.

Conversion and adaptation in Spanish America

The conquest of the Aztec and Inca empires, followed by disease, population collapse, land loss, forced labor, and resettlement, created conditions in which the victors' religion took hold. Europeans read their victory as proof of their God's power, and Native peoples largely agreed. Earlier conquerors like the Aztecs and Incas had also imposed their gods, so joining the winners' religion made practical and spiritual sense. By about 1700 most Native people in Spanish America had been baptized and saw themselves as Christians in some sense. The cost fell hard on women who had served as priests, shamans, and ritual specialists, since an all-male Catholic clergy had no equivalent role for them, and convents were mostly reserved for Spanish women.

Unlike earlier conquerors, the Spanish claimed an exclusive truth and set out to destroy local gods. Missionaries often taught patiently, but frustration sometimes turned violent. In 1535 the bishop of Mexico boasted of destroying 500 shrines and 20,000 idols, and extirpation campaigns in the Andes smashed sacred objects, desecrated ancestors' remains, and publicly humiliated "idolaters." This provoked resistance. In central Peru in the 1560s, the Taki Onqoy ("dancing sickness") movement predicted that the Andean gods, or huacas, would defeat the Christian God and drive out the Spanish. Some Spaniards, notably the missionary Bartolomé de las Casas, argued at court for better treatment of Native Americans.

More common than open revolt was syncretism, the blending of religious traditions. Andean women might offer llama blood to strengthen a village church or make matching cloth for the Virgin Mary and a huaca, and the spirits of the mountains endured. In Mexico, churches were built on old temple sites, lay associations called cofradías organized festivals and funerals, and saints took on the roles of older gods as protectors of the community. The Virgin of Guadalupe, said to have appeared to the Native peasant Juan Diego in 1531, joined Spanish and Mesoamerican ideas of divine motherhood. The result was a distinctly Mexican or Andean Christianity, not a copy of Spain's.

Key points

  • Conquest, disease, and disruption made mass conversion possible.
  • Violent attacks on native religion provoked resistance such as Taki Onqoy.
  • Most Native Christians blended old and new: Guadalupe, saints as local protectors, cofradías.

An Asian comparison: China and the Jesuits

China met Christianity under the powerful, prosperous Ming (1368 to 1644) and Qing (1644 to 1912) dynasties, and the few Europeans there never threatened its independence. Missionaries needed the government's permission to work, so the Jesuits aimed at the educated elite rather than the masses. Following Matteo Ricci (in China 1582 to 1610), they learned Chinese, studied the Confucian classics, and dressed as Confucian scholars. They won attention with European science, mathematics, astronomy, and mapmaking, pointed out parallels between Confucianism and Christianity, and treated ancestor veneration and rites honoring the emperor as civil customs rather than religion. For more than a century Jesuits headed the imperial Bureau of Astronomy.

Results were modest: roughly 200,000 to 300,000 converts over about 250 years, in a population approaching 300 million. Christianity offered little the Chinese felt they needed, since Confucianism, Buddhism, Daoism, and local gods met their spiritual needs, and it asked a lot, such as giving up concubines. In the Chinese Rites Controversy, the pope and rival missionary orders rejected the Jesuits' accommodation and banned Chinese Christians from Confucian and ancestral rites. The pope's claim of authority over Chinese subjects offended Emperor Kangxi, who in 1715 forbade Westerners to spread Christianity. Many missionaries were expelled, and the rest lost favor at court.

Other suspicions hurt the missionaries. Serving the Manchu Qing alienated scholars who saw the dynasty as foreign and illegitimate. Stories of miracles undercut their image as men of science. Some Chinese saw Holy Communion as a kind of cannibalism, and secret Christian meetings resembled the sects that had sparked peasant rebellions. Europeans had also seized the Philippines, so map-loving missionaries could look like spies.

Key points

  • A strong, independent China forced the Jesuits to adapt: elite targets, Confucian dress, science.
  • Few converts, because Christianity met little need and demanded much.
  • The papal ban on Chinese rites led Kangxi to ban missionary preaching.

African religions in the Americas

Europeans were not the only ones whose religions moved and changed. Enslaved Africans carried their religious ideas across the Atlantic. Common West African forms of revelation, such as divination, dream interpretation, visions, and spirit possession, found a place in Africanized versions of Christianity in the Americas. Europeans often saw these practices as sorcery, witchcraft, or devil worship and tried to suppress them.

They survived anyway, in syncretic religions such as Vodou in Haiti, Santería in Cuba, and Candomblé and Macumba in Brazil. Rooted in West African traditions, they featured drumming, ritual dancing, animal sacrifice, and spirit possession. Over time they took on Christian elements such as church attendance, the search for salvation, and candles and crucifixes, and they often identified African spirits with Catholic saints.

Key points

  • African religion traveled with the slave trade.
  • Vodou, Santería, Candomblé, and Macumba blended West African and Catholic elements.
  • Like Native Americans, enslaved Africans reshaped Christianity rather than simply adopting it.

Expansion of the Islamic world

The "long march of Islam," already a thousand years old, continued across sub-Saharan Africa, India, Central Asia, and Southeast Asia. Unlike Christianity in the Americas, Islam usually spread without conquering armies. It was carried by Sufi holy men, Islamic scholars, and traders who posed no threat to local rulers and were often useful to them. They taught Arabic literacy, set up informal schools, provided charms containing Quranic verses, advised rulers, healed the sick, married local people, and generally did not make converts give up older practices. What they offered was a link to the wider, prestigious, and prosperous Islamic world. Islam even reached the Americas: in the early 1800s Muslims led several slave revolts in Brazil.

Conversion usually meant folding Islamic rituals and ideas into local religion, so practice varied widely. In Aceh, a sultanate at the northern tip of Sumatra, rulers enforced Islamic law on diet and almsgiving, and after four women ruled in succession in the late 1600s, women were barred from political power. On Java, by contrast, many women served at royal courts and kept their long-standing role as market traders. Ordinary Javanese combined spirit worship with a tolerant Islam, while merchants tended toward a stricter version closer to Middle Eastern practice.

Key points

  • Islam spread mainly through Sufis, scholars, and traders, not conquest.
  • Converts blended Islam with local religious practice.
  • Southeast Asia shows the range: strict Aceh versus accommodating Java.

Renewal and reform: the Wahhabi movement

To orthodox Muslims, the blending that came with Islamization looked more and more like heresy. In the 1700s, renewal movements across the Islamic world condemned practices that strayed from the Quran and the example of Muhammad. In Mughal India, opposition to policies that accommodated Hindus came to power with Emperor Aurangzeb (r. 1658 to 1707). Religious wars in West Africa targeted corrupt practices and the rulers who allowed them, and in Southeast and Central Asia tension grew between blended local Islam and reformers who wanted a purer, universal faith.

The best-known movement arose in Arabia itself. The scholar Muhammad Ibn Abd al-Wahhab (1703 to 1792) argued that the Islamic world's troubles, such as the weakening of the Ottoman Empire, came from abandoning the pure faith of early Islam. He condemned the veneration of Sufi saints and their tombs, the worship of natural sites, and even the reverence paid to Muhammad's tomb at Medina as idolatry. In the 1740s a local ruler, Muhammad Ibn Saud, backed him, and the movement became an expanding state in central Arabia. It razed tombs, destroyed books on logic, banned tobacco, hashish, and musical instruments, and abolished taxes that religious law did not authorize.

Wahhabism is associated today with strict limits on women, but al-Wahhab himself stressed women's rights under Islamic law: to consent to marriage and set its conditions, to control their dowry, to divorce, and to engage in commerce. The Wahhabi state took Mecca in 1803. An Egyptian army broke its power in 1818, but its ideas kept spreading across the Islamic world, a sign of Islam's continuing vitality.

Key points

  • Renewal movements rejected syncretism and called for a return to the Quran and Muhammad's example.
  • Wahhabism allied with the Saud family to build a state in central Arabia.
  • Its ideas outlived its military defeat in 1818.

China: new directions in an old tradition

Neither China nor India saw anything as dramatic as the Reformation or the Wahhabi movement, and Confucian and Hindu cultures did not spread widely the way Christianity and Islam did. But neither tradition stood still. As in Europe, the growth of commerce and cities, along with political change, encouraged new thinking. Ming and Qing China worked within Neo-Confucianism, a Confucianism enriched by Buddhist and Daoist ideas. The Ming promoted it as a native tradition after Mongol rule, and the Manchu Qing backed it to win over Chinese scholars.

The late Ming thinker Wang Yangming (1472 to 1529) argued that everyone has intuitive moral knowledge ("even robbers know that they should not rob"), so anyone could become virtuous through reflection, without the years of classical study that Confucianism expected of elite "gentlemen." Critics said this bred too much individualism and even helped bring down the Ming. Some Buddhists likewise taught that laypeople could practice at home as monks did. Both ideas resemble Luther's claim that individuals could seek salvation without a priestly hierarchy.

Another movement, kaozheng, or "research based on evidence," aimed to "seek truth from facts." It valued verification, precision, and rigorous analysis and produced works on agriculture, medicine, botany, and crafts. In the Qing era it focused on the critical study of ancient documents, which sometimes undercut Neo-Confucian orthodoxy. It was scientific in method but aimed mostly at the past rather than at nature. Meanwhile, a lively urban popular culture grew up around plays, short stories, how-to painting manuals, and novels such as Cao Xueqin's The Dream of the Red Chamber, a 120-chapter story of an elite family with about 400 characters, most of them women.

Key points

  • Neo-Confucianism remained the framework, backed by both dynasties.
  • Wang Yangming and lay Buddhism stressed individual moral and religious effort.
  • Kaozheng applied evidence-based study, mostly to old texts; popular novels thrived in cities.

India: bridging the Hindu/Muslim divide

In mostly Hindu India, ruled by the Muslim Mughal Empire, new forms of religion brought Hindus and Muslims together. At the elite level, Emperor Akbar (r. 1556 to 1605) created a state cult that combined elements of Islam, Hinduism, and Zoroastrianism. The Mughal court also embraced Renaissance Christian art, with murals of Jesus, Mary, and the saints on palace walls, and it commissioned a Sufi master to write an illustrated book of Hindu yoga postures, the Ocean of Life, to bring yoga into Sufi practice.

In popular culture, bhakti, a devotional Hinduism of songs, prayers, dances, and poetry, sought union with a chosen god. It appealed especially to women, offered a voice for social criticism, and often ignored caste and the rituals of Brahmin priests. Its mysticism had much in common with Sufism, which also stressed direct experience of God, and this helped blur the line between Hindu and Muslim. The beloved poet Mirabai (1498 to 1547), a high-caste woman, broke with convention. Tradition says she refused to burn herself on her husband's funeral pyre (sati), took an untouchable shoemaker as her guru, and wrote poems of longing for the god Krishna.

Sikhism went further, becoming a new religion in the Punjab. Its founder, Guru Nanak (1469 to 1539), came out of the bhakti movement and taught that "there is no Hindu; there is no Muslim; only God." Sikh teaching ignored caste and untouchability, ended the seclusion of women, and stressed the equality of men and women. Drawing converts from both Hindu and Muslim peasants and merchants, the Sikhs became a separate community with their own scripture, the Guru Granth, a center of worship at the Golden Temple of Amritsar, and distinctive dress for men: uncut hair and beards, a turban, and a short sword. Facing hostility from the Mughals and some Hindus in the 1600s, they became a militant community whose soldiers the British later valued.

Key points

  • Akbar's court blended Islam, Hinduism, Zoroastrianism, and Christian art.
  • Bhakti and Sufism both stressed direct experience of God, softening Hindu-Muslim boundaries.
  • Sikhism combined Hindu and Muslim elements into a new faith that rejected caste.

The question of origins: why Europe?

The Scientific Revolution, from the mid-1500s to the early 1700s, replaced reliance on the Bible, the Church, and ancient philosophers with knowledge gained through reason and evidence. Why did it happen in Europe? Between 800 and 1400 the Islamic world had the most advanced science anywhere, in mathematics, astronomy, optics, and medicine, with libraries far larger than Europe's. China had a sophisticated, secular Confucian culture and unmatched technology and economic growth. Neither produced the same breakthrough.

One answer lies in institutions. By the 1100s and 1200s, European law recognized corporations: self-governing groups such as towns, guilds, professional associations, and above all universities like Paris, Bologna, Oxford, Cambridge, and Salamanca. These became "neutral zones" of relative intellectual freedom where the study of nature slowly separated from theology, building on the recently recovered works of Aristotle. Most leaders of the Scientific Revolution came out of them. Islamic colleges, or madrassas, centered on the Quran and religious law and grew suspicious of philosophy and science. China had no independent universities; education prepared students for the Confucian civil service exams.

Europe could also draw on other cultures. Arab medical texts, astronomy, and translations of Greek classics shaped European "natural philosophy" between 1000 and 1500. After 1500 a flood of information about new lands, peoples, plants, animals, and religions reached Europeans, unsettling old certainties and feeding skepticism. The Reformation helped as well, by challenging authority, spreading literacy, and valuing secular work.

Key points

  • Autonomous universities gave European scholars room to study nature.
  • Islamic madrassas and China's exam-centered education left little room for science.
  • Borrowed Islamic learning and new global knowledge shook up European thinking.

Science as cultural revolution

Before the revolution, educated Europeans pictured a motionless earth at the center of the universe, circled by the sun, moon, and stars set in spheres of crystal. It was a universe full of divine purpose, centered on humans and God's plan for their salvation. Nicolaus Copernicus, a Polish astronomer, broke with this picture in On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres (1543), arguing that the sun is at the center and the earth, like the other planets, revolves around it. Johannes Kepler showed that planets move in ellipses, not perfect circles, and in 1609 Galileo built an improved telescope and turned it on the sky. Some thinkers began to imagine an infinite universe in which humanity was a speck of dust.

The high point was Isaac Newton (1642 to 1727), whose laws of motion and theory of universal gravitation showed that the same laws govern a falling apple and the orbiting planets. Heaven and earth were no longer separate realms. The universe now looked like a self-regulating machine, "similar to a clock," as Kepler put it, that could be understood through observation, experiment, and mathematics rather than ancient authority or revelation. René Descartes resolved to seek knowledge only within his own reason or in "the book of nature." Careful dissection did the same for the body, showing the heart to be a pump that circulates blood.

Women were largely shut out of the universities, but a few aristocratic women took part informally. Margaret Cavendish wrote six scientific works and was the only Englishwoman of her century to attend a session of the Royal Society of London. In Germany, Maria Winkelman discovered a comet, but her husband got the credit and the Berlin Academy later refused her a post. The Catholic Church resisted the new ideas: Giordano Bruno was burned at the stake in 1600, and Galileo was forced to recant. Yet the conflict is often exaggerated. None of the early scientists rejected Christianity, Copernicus dedicated his book to the pope, and Newton saw the universe as the work of an intelligent God. Over time, science and religion learned to coexist by keeping to separate spheres.

Key points

  • From an earth-centered universe to a sun-centered machine run by mathematical laws.
  • Knowledge came from observation, experiment, and reason, not authority.
  • Science sometimes clashed with the Church, but early scientists remained Christians.

It replaced an earth-centered, purpose-filled universe guided by God and angels with a sun-centered machine that ran on natural laws, and it made reason and evidence, not the Church or ancient authorities, the source of knowledge.

Zooming in: Galileo and the telescope

The first telescopes were made by Dutch eyeglass makers in the early 1600s, but Galileo Galilei (1564 to 1642) unlocked their potential. Within months of building his improved version he saw craters on the moon, sunspots moving across the sun, the moons of Jupiter, and many new stars. These showed that the heavens were neither perfect nor unchanging and that the cosmos was far larger than anyone had thought. He published the findings in The Starry Messenger (1610), stressing that anyone with a telescope could check them. Even the Church's leading astronomer in Rome, Christoph Clavius, praised his early discoveries.

Galileo concluded that Copernicus was right, but he could not prove it to his critics' satisfaction. When the Church condemned Copernican theory in 1616, it warned him not to teach or promote it. After he published the Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems, which favored the sun-centered model, the Roman Inquisition convicted him of defying the Church's orders. He recanted and spent the rest of his life under house arrest. The deeper question at the trial was what it means to know something. In 1992 Pope John Paul II called the case a "sad misunderstanding" and described scientific and religious knowledge as compatible, a view Galileo himself had held.

Key points

  • The telescope gave anyone visible evidence against the old cosmos.
  • Galileo was convicted for promoting Copernicus against Church orders.
  • The case raised lasting questions about scientific versus religious knowledge.

They show how new instruments and careful observation could overturn long-held ideas, and how that created tension with the Church over who had the authority to decide what was true.

CausationWhy did Galileo get into trouble with the Church?

He published a book favoring Copernicus's sun-centered system after being warned not to promote it, so the Inquisition convicted him of disobeying the Church.

Science and Enlightenment

In the 1700s scientific ideas reached a wider public through cheaper printing, a popular press, rising literacy, and scientific societies, and thinkers began applying reason to human affairs. The Scottish professor Adam Smith described natural laws of the economy. If reason could find the laws of the universe, surely it could find better ways for people to govern themselves. The German philosopher Immanuel Kant summed up the Enlightenment with the motto "Dare to know!": have the courage to use your own understanding. Enlightenment thinkers attacked arbitrary government, the divine right of kings, aristocratic privilege, and religious superstition. John Locke described government as a contract between rulers and ruled, made by people rather than ordained by God, and Montesquieu argued for the separation of powers, or checks and balances.

The French writer Voltaire mocked religious intolerance and, like many of the "enlightened," was a deist: he believed in a distant God, like a clockmaker, who created the world but did not intervene in it. Others became pantheists, equating God with nature. Admiration for China, which seemed to be governed by talented scholar-officials and a rational, secular Confucianism, encouraged thinkers like Voltaire to imagine Europe without supernatural religion. On women, opinion split. Rousseau wrote that women's education should be entirely "relative to men," while the Journal des Dames, the Marquis de Condorcet, and the British writer Mary Wollstonecraft (1792) argued for women's equal rights and rational education.

The central Enlightenment idea, and what made it potentially revolutionary, was progress: society was not fixed by tradition or divine command and could be improved by human reason. Condorcet declared that "the perfectibility of humanity is indefinite," a belief that later inspired revolutions in the Americas, France, Russia, China, and elsewhere. The age also saw a reaction against too much reason. Rousseau valued nature over book learning, the Romantic movement celebrated emotion and imagination, and religious awakenings such as Methodism swept Protestant Europe and North America. "Enlightened" forms of religion appeared too: Quakers stressed tolerance and an "inner light" in everyone, and Unitarians denied the Trinity but honored Jesus as a moral teacher. Religion was challenged, but it persisted and adapted.

Key points

  • The Enlightenment applied scientific reason to government, the economy, religion, and society.
  • Deism and toleration show science reshaping religion.
  • Belief in progress made the Enlightenment potentially revolutionary.

European science beyond the West

In the long run, modern science became Europe's most sought-after cultural export, far more desired than Christianity or democracy. In the early modern era, though, the major Asian societies were interested only modestly and selectively. The telescope, for example, soon reached China, Mughal India, and the Ottoman Empire, but nowhere did it become the "discovery machine" it was in Europe.

Qing China wanted European methods for predicting eclipses, reforming the calendar, and mapping the empire. To make European mathematics acceptable, some Chinese scholars argued that it had grown out of ancient Chinese ideas. European medicine had little influence before the 1800s. Japan had closed itself to most Westerners, allowing only the Dutch to trade at one site near Nagasaki, and it banned Western books until 1720. After that, a small group of scholars translated European works and studied Dutch learning, especially anatomy, since in Japan dissection was work for outcasts. But Dutch learning stayed marginal until Japan was forced open in the mid-1800s.

The powerful Ottoman Empire knew of European science by 1650 but did not translate Copernicus, Kepler, or Newton, and used the new knowledge mainly for maps and calendars. A sun-centered solar system caused little stir. Conservative religious education held back theoretical science of any kind: in 1580 an advanced observatory in Constantinople was torn down after religious scholars blamed an outbreak of plague on efforts to pry into God's secrets. In both Japan and the Ottoman Empire, the systematic adoption of Western science waited until the 1800s, under heavy European pressure.

Key points

  • Asian societies borrowed European science selectively and on their own terms.
  • China: astronomy, calendars, maps, math. Japan: Dutch anatomy. Ottomans: maps and calendars.
  • Wide adoption came only under European pressure in the 1800s.

ComparisonHow did China, Japan, and the Ottoman Empire respond to European science?

All three were selective. China adopted astronomy, calendar, and mapping techniques; a few Japanese scholars studied Dutch anatomy; the Ottomans used it for maps and calendars and did not translate major works. None embraced it widely until the 1800s.

CausationWhy was interest in European science limited in Asia before 1800?

These were powerful, confident societies with their own intellectual traditions that saw no need to adopt European ideas wholesale, and conservative religious or Confucian education gave little room to theoretical science.

Looking ahead: science after 1800

Science kept building on itself and correcting itself, and in the 1800s it was applied in ways that undercut some Enlightenment assumptions. Charles Darwin (The Origin of Species, 1859) argued that all life, humans included, evolves over millions of years through competition and natural selection, an idea that troubled many Christians even more than Copernicus had. Karl Marx saw himself as a scientist of history: conflict between social classes drives change, and socialism, a society without classes, is inevitable.

Darwin and Marx still believed in progress, but driven by struggle rather than reason and education. Sigmund Freud argued that irrational drives toward sex and aggression lie beneath a thin layer of conscience, which challenged the Enlightenment picture of the rational individual. In the 1900s, relativity and quantum theory revised Newton's physics at very small scales and at speeds near that of light.

Key points

  • Darwin, Marx, and Freud applied scientific approaches to life, history, and the mind.
  • They replaced the Enlightenment's rational individual with struggle and irrational drives.
  • Twentieth-century physics revised Newton.

Darwin, Marx, and Freud kept the scientific method and, for Darwin and Marx, a belief in progress, but they saw struggle and irrational drives, not reason and education, as the forces shaping life, history, and the human mind.

Reflections: cultural borrowing and its hazards

Borrowing ran in every direction in this era. Filipinos, Siberians, and many Native Americans took up Christianity; Asian and African peoples adopted Islam; Sikhs drew on both Hinduism and Islam; and Europeans borrowed scientific and medical ideas from the Islamic world, then spread their own science worldwide. In nearly every case borrowing was selective, even under foreign rule. Many converts did not accept the strict exclusiveness of orthodox Christianity or Islam. Chinese elites wanted European maps and mathematics but not medicine, Japanese scholars wanted Dutch anatomy, and neither society adopted Christianity on a wide scale.

Borrowing often caused conflict, especially under foreign domination or threat. Taki Onqoy tried to wipe out Spanish influence, and Chinese and Japanese authorities cracked down on missionaries while still valuing European science and technology. There were also fights over the terms of borrowing: European missionaries and Muslim reformers alike tried to root out "idolatry" among converts. To ease these tensions, people domesticated foreign ideas. The Jesuits stressed parallels between Christianity and Confucianism, Native Americans matched saints with their own gods, and some Mexican churches came to link Catholicism with ancient pre-Aztec communities rather than with the Spanish.

Key points

  • Cultural borrowing was selective, not wholesale.
  • Borrowing sparked conflict over whether, and on what terms, to borrow.
  • Domesticating foreign ideas made them easier to accept.

Primary sources

The chapter's Working with Evidence sources and Historians' Voices, summarized. For each one, think about the author's purpose, audience, and point of view before opening the answers. All of them show Christianity being reshaped as it entered new cultures.

Source 7.1 · Painting, Bolivia, about 1740

La Virgen del Cerro (Virgin Mary of the Mountain)

The Virgin Mary is painted inside the silver mountain of Potosí, her dress shaped like the mountain, with Native miners at work inside it, an Inca ruler at its base, and the pope and Spanish emperor below. Andean religion saw mountains as gods, and the earth mother Pachamama was linked to peaks.

What makes this painting syncretic?

It fuses the Christian Virgin Mary with the Andean belief that mountains embody the divine, and with Pachamama, so Mary and the sacred mountain become one figure.

What might the artist's purpose have been?

To present Christianity in familiar Andean terms, making the faith feel local, while also acknowledging the miners whose labor enriched Spain.

Source 7.2 · Maya text, Yucatán, 18th century

The Chilam Balam of Chumayel

Maya authors wrote in their own language using the Latin alphabet. They tell converts to turn from the old gods, date Jesus's life using the Maya calendar, and lament that Christianity arrived with tribute, church dues, forced debts, and violence, while predicting that God's justice will one day fall on the exploiters.

How does the text blend Maya and Christian ideas?

It tells the Christian story using Maya calendar dates, directions, and colored stones, and it uses the Christian idea of the end times to predict justice for the Maya.

What is the authors' point of view on Spanish rule?

They accept Christianity but sharply criticize the Spanish and the priests for tribute, forced labor, and abuse, a rare Native perspective on conversion.

Source 7.3 · Chinese woodblock print, 17th century

Illustration of the Annunciation

Jesuit books on the life of Christ used prints by Chinese artists. This one sets the angel's visit to Mary in a wealthy scholar's house, with a reading table, a landscape of mountains and a scholar's rock, and clouds like those of Buddhist and Daoist art. Mary could look like a Ming noblewoman or the Buddhist goddess Guanyin.

Why would the Jesuits want a Chinese-style Christian image?

Their strategy was accommodation: presenting Christianity in a familiar elite Chinese setting made it seem less foreign to the scholars they hoped to convert.

Why might the artist have left out the crucifixion scene from the European original?

Execution imagery could repel Chinese viewers, and the Jesuits tended to stress parts of Christianity that fit Chinese values.

Source 7.4 · Papal decree (1715) and Emperor Kangxi's response (1721)

The Chinese Rites Controversy

Pope Clement XI ordered Chinese Catholics to stop honoring Confucius and their ancestors and to avoid certain Chinese words for God, while saying Chinese government and family life should not be disturbed. Kangxi called the decree narrow-minded and ignorant of China and banned Westerners from preaching.

How would the pope's ban make life harder for Chinese converts?

Ancestor rites and Confucian ceremonies were central to family duty and to officials' public roles, so converts would have to cut themselves off from Chinese society.

What does Kangxi's reaction reveal?

He saw the decree as foreign interference in Chinese culture and in his authority, which shows why the Jesuits' accommodation had been necessary for any missionary work.

Source 7.5 · Mughal painting, early 17th century

The Holy Family

Akbar and Jahangir invited Jesuits to court and collected European religious art. Mughal artists painted Christian scenes in Indian style, and images of Jesus and Mary appeared even near the throne and on the imperial seal. Jesus and Mary were honored in Islam, but almost no one at court converted.

Why did Mughal rulers embrace Christian art without converting?

Jesus and Mary already had honored places in Islam, the art enhanced the emperors' prestige, and it fit their effort to build a tolerant, blended elite culture.

What does this image share with the Chinese print?

Both place Christian figures in the elite homes and dress of their own society, adapting the faith to local culture.

Voice 7.1 · Historian, 2015

Merry Wiesner-Hanks on the Virgin of Guadalupe

Wiesner-Hanks presents two views. In the traditional account, Mary appeared to Juan Diego in 1531, and her shrine became a symbol of protection for Native and mixed-race Mexicans. Skeptics note that written accounts came a century later and that the hill was once a shrine to the Aztec mother goddess Coatlicue, so the Church may have invented the story to take over an Aztec holy site.

How does each interpretation change our view of how Mexico was Christianized?

If the vision is genuine, it shows Native people shaping Christianity from below. If the Church invented it, it shows missionaries deliberately using syncretism to replace Aztec worship.

Voice 7.2 · Historian, 2009

Diarmaid MacCulloch on Jesuit strategies in China

MacCulloch notes that Ricci first dressed as a Buddhist monk before learning that elites looked down on monks, then switched to Confucian scholar's dress. Court favor came from science, but most converts were ordinary people, served by very few priests and by local lay leaders: catechists and consecrated laywomen who taught women and children.

What challenges did the Jesuits face, and how did they respond?

They faced language barriers, few priests, and a culture that respected only learned men. They adapted by dressing and studying as Confucian scholars, using science to win court favor, and relying on Chinese lay leaders.

In Mughal India, Hindus lived under Muslim rule, and the bhakti movement and Sufism were already blurring the line between the two faiths. Guru Nanak, influenced by bhakti, taught that there was "no Hindu, no Muslim, only God," rejected caste, and drew followers from both religions, who formed a distinct community with its own scripture, the Guru Granth, and the Golden Temple at Amritsar.

Show how it's scored

3 points. 1 point: the context (Mughal rule over a Hindu majority, bhakti and Sufi mysticism bridging the faiths, Akbar's blended court culture). 1 point: Guru Nanak's teaching joining Hindu and Muslim ideas. 1 point: a feature of the new community (Guru Granth, Golden Temple, rejection of caste, equality of men and women, dress for men, later militancy). Reject: claims that Sikhism was a branch of Christianity or was founded by Akbar.

Causation Explain why the Scientific Revolution occurred in Europe rather than in China or the Islamic world. 4 points

Show a model answer

Europe's legal system protected self-governing corporations, including universities like Paris and Oxford, which became neutral zones where scholars could study nature with relative freedom; in contrast, madrassas focused on the Quran and grew suspicious of science, and China's education served the Confucian civil service exams. Europe could also draw on Arab science and translations of Greek works, and after 1500 a flood of new global knowledge undermined old authorities and encouraged skepticism.

Show how it's scored

4 points: 1 point each for up to four accurate, explained reasons: autonomous universities and the corporation idea; the scientific core of the university curriculum built on Aristotle; madrassas' religious focus and suspicion of science; China's exam-centered education and lack of independent universities; borrowing from Islamic science; new knowledge from global exploration; the Reformation's challenge to authority and promotion of literacy. Reject: claims that the Islamic world or China lacked any science.

Continuity and change Explain how the Scientific Revolution changed European understandings of the universe. 4 points

Show a model answer

Before, Europeans believed in a stationary earth at the center of a universe of crystal spheres, guided by God and angels. Copernicus put the sun at the center, Kepler showed elliptical orbits, Galileo's telescope revealed an imperfect and vast cosmos, and Newton's universal gravitation showed that one set of laws governed heaven and earth. The universe came to be seen as a self-regulating machine knowable by reason and observation rather than by Church authority.

Show how it's scored

4 points: 1 point for the older view (earth-centered, crystal spheres, divine purpose). Up to 2 points for specific changes, 1 each (Copernicus; Kepler; Galileo; Newton; Descartes; the body as a machine). 1 point for the broader shift (a mechanical universe; reason and evidence replacing authority). Reject: claims that the Church taught a sun-centered universe before Copernicus.

Causation Explain how Enlightenment thinkers applied ideas from the Scientific Revolution to human society. 4 points

Show a model answer

Enlightenment thinkers reasoned that if science could find the natural laws of the universe, reason could also find laws for human society. Adam Smith described natural laws of the economy, John Locke described government as a human-made contract rather than divine right, Montesquieu proposed checks and balances, and Voltaire attacked religious intolerance. Above all, they believed society could progress through reason, an idea that later inspired revolutions.

Show how it's scored

4 points: 1 point for the link (applying reason and natural law to human affairs). Up to 3 points for specific applications, 1 each: Smith and the economy; Locke and contract government; Montesquieu and separation of powers; Voltaire and toleration; deism; Condorcet and progress; debates about women's education. Reject: claims that the Enlightenment rejected reason.

Comparison Compare how TWO of these societies responded to European science before 1800: China, Japan, and the Ottoman Empire. 4 points

Show a model answer

Similarity: both China and the Ottoman Empire took only practical pieces of European science, such as calendars and maps, because they were powerful, confident societies with strong intellectual traditions of their own. Difference: China actively used Jesuit astronomers and adapted European mathematics by tracing it to ancient Chinese ideas, while the Ottomans did not even translate Copernicus, Kepler, or Newton, and religious conservatives dismantled an observatory in Constantinople.

Show how it's scored

4 points: 2 for the similarity and 2 for the difference, each needing BOTH societies. Similarities: selective borrowing; practical rather than philosophical interest; strong, independent states; wide adoption only after European pressure in the 1800s. Differences: China's use of Jesuit astronomers and calendar reform; Japan's Dutch learning and focus on anatomy; Japan's book ban until 1720; the Ottoman refusal to translate major works and the 1580 observatory. Reject: claims that any of the three embraced European science wholesale before 1800.

Argument To what extent were the cultural changes of 1450 to 1750 the result of cross-cultural interaction? 4 points

Show a model answer

To a large extent. Christianity became a global religion through European empire, and converts in the Americas and Asia blended it with local beliefs; Sikhism combined Hindu and Muslim teachings; and Europe's Scientific Revolution drew on Islamic science and new knowledge from around the world. However, some major changes grew mainly from within: the Reformation came from tensions inside European Christianity, and Wahhabism, Wang Yangming's thought, and kaozheng arose from debates within Islamic and Confucian traditions.

Show how it's scored

4 points. 1 point: a clear claim about the extent. Up to 2 points: specific evidence of cross-cultural causes (global Christianity and syncretism; African religions in the Americas; Sikhism; Islamic and global knowledge in European science; Voltaire's admiration of China; Jesuit science in China). 1 point: evidence of internally driven change that qualifies the claim (the Reformation; Wahhabism; Wang Yangming; kaozheng; Chinese popular novels). Reject: answers with no specific evidence.

Big picture questions

These pull the whole chapter together. Outline an answer, then compare it with the suggested points.

Why did Christianity take hold in some places more than in others?

It spread most where European power was overwhelming and no literate world religion existed: Spanish America and the Philippines. In China, India, Japan, and the Islamic world, strong states and established Confucian, Buddhist, Hindu, and Islamic traditions limited it.

In what ways was the missionary message of Christianity reshaped by the cultures of Asian and American peoples?

Native Americans merged saints with older gods, created figures like the Virgin of Guadalupe, and kept huacas and old rituals. In China, Christianity was presented through Confucian ideas and Chinese art, and some heard eternal life as Daoist immortality. Mughal artists painted the Holy Family as Indian elites.

In what ways did the spread of Christianity, Islam, and modern science give rise to culturally based conflicts?

Christianity provoked the Reformation's religious wars, extirpation campaigns and Taki Onqoy in the Andes, and the Chinese Rites Controversy. Islamic syncretism provoked Wahhabi and other reform movements. Science clashed with the Church over Galileo and Bruno, and with conservative Ottoman scholars over the Constantinople observatory.

How might you challenge a Eurocentric view of the early modern era while acknowledging Europe's growing role?

Europeans spread Christianity and created modern science, but Asians, Africans, and Native Americans decided how to accept, reject, or blend Christianity; Islam kept expanding on its own; China and India produced their own new thinking; and European science drew on Islamic learning and knowledge from the wider world.
