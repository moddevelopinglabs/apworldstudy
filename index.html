Commerce and Consequence

AP World History, Chapter 6. How spices, silver, fur, and enslaved people tied the early modern world together, about 1450 to 1750.

Work through it in order: read a section's summary, try its margin questions before opening the answers, and mark it as reviewed. Then use the comparison charts and short answer practice, and finish with the quiz.

The big picture

From about 1450 to 1750, commerce joined empire as one of the two forces that tied the world together. Four flows define the chapter: Europeans forced their way into the old spice trade of the Indian Ocean; silver from the Americas and Japan poured into a world economy centered on China; furs from North America and Siberia fed markets in Europe and China; and millions of enslaved Africans were shipped across the Atlantic to work on American plantations.

Europeans were prominent, but they were far from the only actors. Asian merchants and states, African rulers and traders, and Native American hunters all shaped these exchanges, sometimes on their own terms. The results were uneven: trade made some people rich, impoverished or enslaved others, and changed environments from the Spice Islands to the Great Lakes.

Four themes to remember

  • Europeans used force to break into Asian trade but often ended up fitting into older patterns.
  • Silver made trade truly global, and China's demand for it drove the whole system.
  • The fur trade brought Native Americans new goods along with devastating losses.
  • The Atlantic slave trade ran on European demand and African supply, with lasting effects on three continents.

Why Europeans went east

Columbus reached the Americas by accident, but Vasco da Gama's arrival in India was the payoff of a deliberate Portuguese effort that crept down the African coast, rounded its southern tip, and crossed the Indian Ocean. What Europeans wanted most was spices, pepper above all, along with Asian silks, cottons, and gems.

Europe could reach outward because it was recovering from the Black Death: its population and cities were growing, its monarchies taxed better and fielded gunpowder armies, and commercial cities were building market economies. Two problems pushed Europeans to find a sea route. Asian goods reached them through Muslim traders in Egypt and Venetian merchants who controlled the trade, which Europeans resented for economic, political, and religious reasons, including hopes of joining the legendary Christian king Prester John against Islam. And Europe made little that Asians wanted, so it paid in gold and silver, a trade deficit that fueled the hunt for precious metals.

Key points

  • Spices were the main motive: seasoning, preservatives, medicine, and supposed aphrodisiacs.
  • Going around Muslim and Venetian middlemen was both an economic and a religious goal.
  • Europe's trade deficit with Asia explains its hunger for gold and, later, American silver.

The Portuguese: an empire of commerce

The Indian Ocean world the Portuguese entered was vast and diverse. East Africans, Arabs, Persians, Indians, Malays, and Chinese traded there, most of them Muslim but alongside Hindus, Buddhists, Christians, and Jews. Portuguese goods couldn't compete, but their ships carried cannons, local merchant ships were lightly armed, and no great power had patrolled the sea since China pulled back its fleets.

Using that edge, the Portuguese seized fortified bases at Mombasa, Hormuz, Goa, and Malacca and won Macao from China through bribery and negotiation. Resistance met brutal force: at Mombasa they burned the city and killed about 1,500 people. Historians call the result a trading post empire, built to control commerce rather than land. Ships had to buy a pass called a cartaz and pay duties on their cargo.

It fell short. The Portuguese never controlled much more than half the spice trade to Europe, and older routes through the Ottoman Empire revived. They ended up selling shipping services between Asian ports, the carrying trade, and many settled in, married Asian women, learned local languages, and sometimes became Muslims. Overextended and resisted by Japan, Burma, Mughal India, Persia, Oman, and rival Europeans, the network was in steep decline by about 1600.

Key points

  • Military technology, not better goods, got the Portuguese into Asian trade.
  • Trading post empire: control trade through forts and passes, not territory.
  • In the end they adapted to Asian trade more than they transformed it.

Spain and the Philippines

Spain challenged Portugal by taking the Philippines, named for King Philip II. Three conditions invited outright conquest instead of a string of trading posts: the islands were close to China and the Spice Islands, they were divided into small, competing chiefdoms, and no major power claimed them. Organized largely from Mexico, the takeover relied on small forces, gunpowder, local alliances, gifts to chiefs, and Catholic ceremony, and it was often bloodless.

Missionaries made the Philippines Asia's only major Christian society, though Islam on the southern island of Mindanao sustained centuries of resistance. Familiar Spanish practices from the Americas followed: people were moved into Christian towns, tribute and unpaid labor were imposed, large estates appeared, and male priests pushed aside women who had served as ritual specialists, healers, and midwives.

Manila grew into a diverse, prosperous colonial capital with thousands of Japanese and tens of thousands of Chinese residents. The Spanish depended on Chinese traders and artisans but resented their success and refusal to convert, which led to discrimination, expulsions, and massacres.

Key points

  • Colonial rule, not a trading post empire, because local societies were small and weak.
  • Asia's only major Christian outpost, with Muslim resistance on Mindanao.
  • Manila linked the Spanish colony to China's huge economy.

ComparisonWhy did Spain build a colony in the Philippines when Portugal built only trading posts?

The Philippines had small, divided chiefdoms, no rival claims, and a location near China and the Spice Islands, so conquest was easy. Portugal faced larger, wealthier societies where controlling trade was the realistic goal.

ComparisonHow did Spanish rule in the Philippines resemble Spanish rule in the Americas?

Both involved missionary conversion, moving people into towns, tribute and forced labor, large estates, and the loss of women's traditional religious roles.

The East India companies

In the early 1600s the Dutch and English overtook the Portuguese, often by force, while competing fiercely with each other. Both organized their ventures as private joint-stock companies that pooled many investors' money and spread the risk. Government charters gave the British and Dutch East India Companies trading monopolies plus the power to make war and govern, so they acted almost like states. The Dutch focused on Indonesia and the English on India; a French company came later.

In Indonesia, where states were small and weak, the Dutch seized control of spice production itself, not just shipping. On the Banda Islands, famous for nutmeg, they killed, enslaved, or starved nearly the whole population and replaced it with Dutch planters using enslaved workers. For a time they monopolized nutmeg, mace, and cloves and sold them for many times what they paid, while the islands' economies collapsed.

The British, less well funded and shut out of the spice islands, turned to India. There the powerful Mughal Empire meant they had to trade by permission, paying fees and bribes for bases at Bombay, Calcutta, and Madras. Cotton textiles became their main product, and whole villages specialized in making cloth for the British market. Both companies joined the carrying trade, shifted to bulk goods for mass markets such as pepper, textiles, tea, and coffee, and eventually turned their trading bases into colonial rule over India and Indonesia.

Key points

  • Joint-stock companies with state charters: private businesses with the powers of a government.
  • Dutch: violent control of spice production. British: trade by permission in Mughal India.
  • Both moved toward mass-market goods and, later, territorial rule.

ComparisonHow did the Dutch and British East India Companies operate differently?

The Dutch used force to control spice production in weak Indonesian states, as on the Banda Islands. The British couldn't challenge the Mughals, so they traded by permission and payment in India and focused on cotton textiles.

Continuity and changeHow much did the companies change the Asian societies they dealt with?

In the spice islands, the change was devastating: populations destroyed and economies shattered. In India the British depended on Mughal permission, though some villages reorganized around making textiles. Bigger changes came later, when both companies became colonial rulers.

Asians and Asian commerce

Europeans mattered far less in Asia than in the Americas or Africa. They ruled only the Philippines, parts of Java, and a few spice islands. Siam expelled the French for aggressive missionary work, and to the great powers of Mughal India, China, and Japan, Europeans were minor players in large, prosperous economies.

Japan shows how an Asian state could control the newcomers. While Japan was divided among warring lords, the daimyo, Europeans were welcomed for their guns, ships, knowledge, trade, and even religion, and about 300,000 Japanese became Christian. Once the Tokugawa shoguns unified the country, they saw Europeans as a threat: they expelled missionaries, executed converts, banned Japanese from going abroad, and let only the Dutch trade at the small island of Dejima. That narrow channel still brought in Western science, called Rangaku or Dutch learning, while Japan kept trading with China, Korea, and Southeast Asia.

Asian merchants kept thriving. Japanese traders operated, sometimes violently, in Southeast Asia, but unlike European states the shogun disowned them. Chinese merchants dominated trade between Southeast Asia and China, Southeast Asian women remained active traders, Armenian Christians based in New Julfa traded across Eurasia, and Indian merchants and moneylenders worked across Central Asia, Persia, and Russia. Overland trade stayed in Asian hands, and Indian firms like Virji Vora's could dictate prices to European companies and lend them money at high interest.

Key points

  • European territorial control in Asia was very limited.
  • Japan welcomed Europeans, then tightly restricted them.
  • Asian commercial networks were as sophisticated as Europe's and kept operating.

Silver and global commerce

Silver, even more than spices, created the first truly global trade network. Huge deposits were found in Bolivia and Japan at about the same time, and Spanish America produced most of the world's silver. Each year Spanish ships carried silver from Acapulco across the Pacific to Manila, the first lasting direct link between the Americas and Asia.

China drove the system. When China required its huge population to pay taxes in silver, the metal's value there soared, so anyone with silver could buy far more Chinese silk and porcelain. Silver flowed to China from every direction, through Manila, on European ships carrying Japanese silver, and through Europe's purchases of Asian goods. Historians call this the silver drain, and the Spanish piece of eight became a currency used from the Americas to India and China.

Its effects differed by place. Potosi in Bolivia grew into the largest city in the Americas, with a wealthy elite, deadly forced labor and mercury poisoning for Native American miners, deforestation and flooding, and business openings for women who ran shops, bakeries, and taverns. Spain's Crown grew rich, but silver brought inflation instead of real growth in an economy weighed down by monopolies, an aristocracy that scorned business, and costly religious crusading, so Spain lost its lead in Europe. Across Europe, silver-driven inflation fed the unrest of the General Crisis.

Japan's shoguns used silver to unify the country, work with merchants, invest in farming and industry, and protect forests, laying foundations for later industrialization. In China, silver deepened commercialization and regional specialization, while southern China lost much of its forest. Economically, Europeans were mostly middlemen in an Asia-centered world economy where Chinese silks and Indian cottons outsold European cloth.

Key points

  • China's silver tax made China the center of global silver demand.
  • Silver enriched Spain's rulers but not its economy; Japan used it far more productively.
  • Asia remained the heart of the world economy, with Europeans as middlemen.

It was the first commodity traded on a truly global scale, linking the Americas, Europe, Africa, and Asia, and it shows that China's economy, not Europe's, was the center of early modern trade.

Spain's Crown grew rich but its economy stagnated under inflation and rigid institutions. Japan's rulers used silver to unify the country and build a commercial economy while protecting forests. China's economy became more commercialized and specialized but suffered heavy deforestation.

The world hunt for fur

Furs became a major global commodity alongside silver, spices, and textiles. Europe's own fur-bearing animals had been hunted out, and the cold winters of the Little Ice Age raised demand. In North America the French traded along the St. Lawrence, the Great Lakes, and the Mississippi, the British around Hudson Bay, and the Dutch along the Hudson River, while the southern colonies shipped huge numbers of deerskins to England's leather industry.

Native Americans did the trapping and processing and traded furs for guns, blankets, metal tools, rum, and brandy. Their labor was cheap but not coerced, and at first trade brought real benefits: the Hurons exchanged tens of thousands of pelts a year, chiefs gained goods to hand out, and European competition kept prices fair. But the costs mounted. European diseases killed about half the Hurons; warfare grew deadlier with guns, mourning wars to capture replacements for the dead, and the long French-British rivalry; dependence on European goods wiped out traditional crafts; overhunting nearly wiped out beavers and depleted deer; and alcohol devastated communities. Some peoples, like the Iroquois, also sold ginseng to China, rented land, and worked for wages.

Women's lives changed too. Many native women married traders by the custom of the country, gaining influence as guides and go-betweens but sometimes being abandoned. Among the Ojibwa, women lost authority as their work shifted to processing furs, yet they controlled the trade in wild rice and maple syrup that traders needed.

In Siberia, furs, the soft gold, were the main reason for Russian expansion. Native Siberians suffered disease, dependence, settler encroachment, and depleted animals as in North America, but Russia faced no competing powers, forced every adult Siberian man to pay tribute in furs, took hostages to enforce it, and sent its own trappers to compete with local hunters.

Key points

  • Native Americans were active traders, not forced laborers, but disease, dependence, war, and alcohol took a huge toll.
  • The environmental cost was steep: beavers and deer were hunted toward extinction.
  • In Siberia, a single empire collected furs as forced tribute.

Early benefits included new goods and stronger chiefs, but disease, deadlier warfare, dependence on European goods, lost crafts, overhunting, and alcohol devastated many societies.

Hunting was men's work, so men's status rose and women's household production declined, as among the Ojibwa. Some women gained influence by marrying traders or controlling the wild rice and maple syrup trade.

The slave trade in context

No commercial link had deeper human consequences than the transatlantic slave system. About 12.5 million Africans were forced onto ships, and about 10.7 million survived the Atlantic crossing; many more died during capture and the march to the coast. Despite the language of trade, it rested on violence: capture, branding, chains, forced labor, and broken families.

Slavery itself was ancient. Before 1500 the Mediterranean and Indian Ocean were the main slave systems, and trans-Saharan and East African trades sent Africans into the Islamic world. There, most enslaved people worked in households, women were preferred about two to one, and some slaves rose to military or political power. Atlantic slavery was different: it was enormous, central to plantation economies, inherited with little hope of freedom, sharply at odds with ideals of liberty, and tied entirely to Africa and race.

Its model came from Mediterranean sugar plantations that Europeans learned about from the Arabs, a capital-intensive, almost factory-like industry. Slavs from the Black Sea first supplied the labor, which is where the word slave comes from, until the Ottoman capture of Constantinople cut that off. Africa became the source largely by elimination: Native Americans died of disease, poor Europeans were Christians and supposedly exempt, and indentured servants were costly and temporary, while Africans were skilled farmers with some immunity, non-Christian, relatively close, and available through African networks. Historians debate whether European racism came from Muslim views of Black Africans or from English views of the Irish, but slavery and racism soon reinforced each other.

Key points

  • Atlantic slavery was unique in its scale, inherited status, plantation labor, and racial basis.
  • Its roots lay in Mediterranean sugar plantations.
  • Africa became the labor source through a process of elimination.

The slave trade in practice

European demand caused the trade, and Europeans controlled it from the African coast to the American plantation. Inside Africa, though, African merchants and rulers controlled capture and sale. Europeans waited on their ships or in coastal forts, almost never tried to conquer, and dealt with African authorities as equals; an English agent who insulted the king of Niumi was jailed until ransomed. African sellers wanted European and Indian textiles, cowrie shells, metal goods, guns, tobacco, alcohol, and beads, and Europeans bought some of these with American silver, tying the slave trade to the global trade in silver and cloth.

The trade was small at first, when the Portuguese cared as much about gold and acted as coastal carriers, then grew as the British, Dutch, and French joined in, peaking as American plantations boomed. Captives came mostly from West and South-Central Africa and were usually outsiders to the sellers' communities, such as prisoners of war, criminals, and debtors; West Africans had no shared African identity. Most were taken to Brazil and the Caribbean.

More than 14 percent died during the Middle Passage, and about one voyage in ten saw a major revolt. Resistance continued in the Americas through slowdowns, escape, and rebellion. Runaways built maroon societies such as Palmares in Brazil, home to 10,000 or more people, but only the Haitian Revolution won lasting freedom through a full-scale revolt.

Key points

  • European demand, African supply: Africans controlled capture and sale.
  • The trade connected to global silver and Asian textiles.
  • Enslaved people resisted constantly, from shipboard revolts to maroon communities.

Effects on Africa

The slave trade made Africa a permanent part of the Atlantic world. For a long time, enslaved Africans arriving in the Americas outnumbered European migrants three or four to one. Africa had no population collapse like the Great Dying, but its growth slowed while Europe and China expanded. Its economy stagnated because the elites who profited didn't invest in production, and the trade bred corruption, as courts were manipulated to supply victims. American crops like maize and cassava added food, but the world wanted Africa's people, not its products.

Women felt the trade in many ways. With far more men shipped away, women's workloads grew, polygyny increased, and elites kept more female slaves at home. A few women gained power: the signares of Senegambia married European merchants and ran their own trading networks, royal women helped hold together the kingdom of Dahomey, women held offices in Kongo, and Queen Nzinga of Matamba resisted Portuguese expansion.

Effects on states varied. Small, kinship-based societies without strong rulers suffered constant raids. Large kingdoms like Kongo and Oyo slowly broke apart as outlying regions gained guns and trade. Benin limited the trade and for a long time banned the export of male slaves, selling pepper and cotton cloth instead, until changing conditions pushed it into limited participation. Dahomey went the other way, building a state whose army raided for slaves every year and whose government depended on the trade. In the Americas, the African diaspora brought African religion, music, art, and food into new cultures, and slavery later became a metaphor for many kinds of oppression.

Key points

  • Slowed population growth, economic stagnation, and corruption.
  • Mixed effects on women: heavier burdens for most, power for a few.
  • States responded differently: Benin limited the trade, while Dahomey built itself on it.

The Atlantic world debate

Since the 1970s, many historians have studied an Atlantic world, the web of exchange linking Europe, Africa, and the Americas. The idea frees history from national borders, invites comparison between empires, and gives context to places like the Caribbean, whose history depends on links to Africa, Europe, and North America. It also corrects assumptions: fewer than 5 percent of enslaved Africans went to North America.

Critics argue there was never one Atlantic world but many, divided by empire, religion, and race, including a Black Atlantic, and that the Atlantic was tightly tied to Asia through silver, Indian textiles, and cowrie shells from the Maldives. Other debates focus on agency, meaning who made things happen: not only Europeans, but also disease, indigenous allies of the Spanish, African rulers who supplied captives, and enslaved and colonized people who resisted and reshaped Christianity. Historians also argue about impact and moral responsibility.

Key points

  • The Atlantic world idea looks past nations to shared flows of people, goods, and disease.
  • Critics stress its many divisions and its ties to Asia.
  • Agency belonged to many groups, not just Europeans.

Zooming in: Ayuba Suleiman Diallo

Ayuba was a young, literate Muslim from a family of scholars between the Gambia and Senegal rivers. Sent to sell some of his family's slaves to an English captain, he traded them elsewhere instead, then was captured on the way home and sold to that same captain. Enslaved on a Maryland tobacco farm, he ran away and was jailed, where a lawyer recognized him as an educated Muslim and helped bring him to England. Philanthropists bought his freedom, and English elites welcomed him.

Back home, he found his family waiting and resumed his old life, including owning slaves, while keeping ties to the English Royal African Company. His story complicates simple accounts of the slave trade: slavery was familiar in his world, and he moved between enslaver and enslaved.

Key points

  • Slavery was a normal institution in his society, practiced by elites like his family.
  • Anyone outside the protection of their community could be captured and sold.

Globalization then and now

Early modern trade looks surprisingly modern: goods circulated worldwide, a Spanish silver coin worked as an international currency, people produced for world markets, and private companies backed by governments operated globally. Wealthy Europeans ate from Chinese porcelain, wore Indian cotton, and drank Mexican chocolate, Chinese tea, and Yemeni coffee sweetened with Caribbean or Brazilian sugar.

The differences matter just as much. Trade was far smaller and slower, it ran on muscle, wind, and water rather than industrial power, its center was Asia and especially China rather than Europe and North America, and it depended on empire and slavery, both later discredited.

Key points

  • Similar: global goods, an international currency, world markets, and global companies.
  • Different: scale, speed, preindustrial power, an Asian center, and dependence on empire and slavery.

Causation Explain TWO reasons Europeans sought a direct sea route into Asian commerce. 4 points

Show a model answer

Europeans wanted direct access to tropical spices, especially pepper, along with Asian silks and cottons. They also wanted to bypass the Muslim traders and the city of Venice that controlled the flow of Eastern goods into Europe, since relying on them was costly and, for the Portuguese, a religious and political problem as well.

Show how it's scored

4 points: 2 per reason. For each reason, 1 point for a specific, accurate cause and 1 point for explaining how it pushed Europeans toward Asia. Accept: demand for spices (pepper, cinnamon, nutmeg, cloves) and other Asian goods; bypassing Muslim and Venetian middlemen; the hope of allying with Prester John against Islam; the trade deficit and the search for gold and silver to pay for Asian goods; Europe's recovery after the Black Death (growing populations, cities, stronger monarchies, commercial economies). The two reasons must be genuinely different. Reject: claims that Europeans wanted to escape the Mongols or that Asia was buying European manufactured goods.

Comparison Compare how the Portuguese and the Spanish operated in Asia. Give one similarity and one difference. 4 points

Show a model answer

Similarity: both used gunpowder weapons and force to establish themselves in Asia, and both were Catholic powers seeking a share of Asian trade. Difference: the Portuguese tried to control commerce through a network of fortified bases like Goa and Malacca rather than ruling territory, while the Spanish set up outright colonial rule over the Philippines, converting much of the population to Christianity and imposing tribute and labor.

Show how it's scored

4 points: 2 for the similarity and 2 for the difference. For each, 1 point for an accurate, specific point and 1 point for showing it for BOTH powers. Similarities: use of force and gunpowder weapons; Catholic powers; seeking trade with Asia and access to China and the Spice Islands. Differences: Portuguese trading post empire (fortified bases, the cartaz, control of commerce rather than land) versus Spanish colonial rule over the Philippines (conquest from Mexico, mass conversion, tribute, forced relocation, landed estates); Portuguese assimilating into Asian trade versus Spain creating Asia's only major Christian society. Reject: claims that Portugal ruled large Asian territories or that Spain built a trading post empire in India.

Comparison Compare the Dutch and British East India Companies in Asia. Give one similarity and one difference. 4 points

Show a model answer

Similarity: both were private joint-stock companies with government charters that gave them trading monopolies and the power to make war and govern, and both eventually turned their trading networks into colonial rule. Difference: the Dutch used violence to control both the production and shipping of spices in Indonesia, as on the nutmeg islands, while the British, kept out of the Spice Islands and facing the powerful Mughal Empire, had to secure trading bases in India with permission and bribes and focused on cotton textiles.

Show how it's scored

4 points: 2 for the similarity and 2 for the difference. For each, 1 point for an accurate, specific point and 1 point for showing it for BOTH companies. Similarities: private joint-stock companies; royal charters with monopolies and power to make war and govern; displacing the Portuguese; the carrying trade within Asia; bulk goods for a mass market; later colonial rule (the Dutch in Indonesia, the British in India). Differences: Dutch 'trade by warfare' and control of spice production in Indonesia versus British trade by permission and payment in Mughal India; the Dutch focus on spices versus the British focus on cotton textiles; the Dutch being better financed. Reject: claims that the British conquered the Mughals in this period or that the Dutch focused on India.

Continuity and change To what extent did Europeans transform Asian commerce, and in what ways did they fit into older patterns? 4 points

Show a model answer

Europeans changed Asian commerce by militarizing the sea lanes with armed ships, demanding passes and duties, and, in the Dutch case, violently seizing control of spice production. Yet much stayed the same: Europeans never controlled more than about half the spice trade, older routes revived, Asian merchants like Chinese, Indian, and Armenian traders kept thriving, overland trade stayed in Asian hands, and Europeans fit into the old system by selling shipping services in the carrying trade and marrying into local society.

Show how it's scored

4 points: 2 for change and 2 for continuity, 1 point per accurate, specific example. Change: armed ships and naval dominance; the cartaz and duties; Dutch control of spice production and the Banda massacre; Spanish colonial rule in the Philippines; bulk trade for mass markets; later colonial rule in India and Indonesia. Continuity: Portuguese control of no more than about half the spice trade; revival of older routes; continued activity of Asian merchants (Chinese, Indian, Armenian, Southeast Asian women); overland trade in Asian hands; Europeans joining the carrying trade and assimilating; the strength of Mughal India, China, and Japan. Reject: claims that Europeans conquered most of Asia in this era.

Context Explain how Japan's rulers controlled contact with Europeans, and why they did so. 3 points

Show a model answer

Once the Tokugawa shoguns unified Japan, they saw Europeans and Christianity as a threat to the country's new unity. They expelled missionaries, violently suppressed Christianity, banned Japanese from traveling abroad, and kept out most European traders, allowing only the Dutch to trade at a single site, while still trading with China, Korea, and Southeast Asia.

Show how it's scored

3 points. 1 point: the reason (the shoguns' fear that Europeans and Christianity threatened Japan's newly established unity). Up to 2 points: specific measures, 1 each (expelling missionaries and suppressing Christianity; banning travel abroad; banning most European traders; allowing only the Dutch at one site; keeping trade with China, Korea, and Southeast Asia). Reject: claims that Japan cut off all foreign trade or that the Portuguese were the only Europeans allowed to stay.

Connection Explain how China's demand for silver shaped global trade. 4 points

Show a model answer

When China required its huge population to pay taxes in silver, the value of silver there skyrocketed, so foreigners with silver could buy far more Chinese silk and porcelain. Silver from Bolivia and Mexico was shipped across the Pacific to the Philippines and across the Atlantic to Europe and then on to Asia, while Japanese silver also went to China. As a result, most of the world's silver ended up in China, creating the first truly global trade network, with Europeans acting mainly as middlemen.

Show how it's scored

4 points. 1 point: China's policy requiring taxes to be paid in silver and the resulting rise in silver's value. Up to 2 points: specific routes or flows, 1 each (Bolivian silver shipped from Mexico across the Pacific to the Philippine capital; silver crossing the Atlantic and passing through Europe to Asia; Japanese silver carried to China; the Spanish silver coin used worldwide). 1 point: an explained global effect (the first genuinely global trade network, Europeans as middlemen, Asian centrality in the world economy). Reject: claims that most silver stayed in Spain or that China exported silver to Europe.

Comparison Compare how the silver boom affected Spain and Japan. Give one similarity and one difference. 4 points

Show a model answer

Similarity: in both Spain and Japan, silver greatly enriched the rulers and paid for their political and military goals. Difference: Spain's silver mostly caused inflation in a rigid economy run by monopolies and a leisure-loving aristocracy, so Spain lost its dominance, while Japan's shoguns used silver to unify the country, allied with merchants, invested in agriculture and industry, and protected their forests.

Show how it's scored

4 points: 2 for the similarity and 2 for the difference. For each, 1 point for an accurate, specific point and 1 point for showing it in BOTH countries. Similarities: silver enriched the rulers or state; silver funded military and political ambitions. Differences: inflation and stagnation in Spain versus a growing commercial economy in Japan; Spain's rigid economy, monopolies, aristocratic disdain for enterprise, and religious crusading versus Japan's alliance with merchants and investment in agriculture and industry; Spain's decline versus Japan's unification; Japan's forest conservation and population control. Reject: claims that Spain industrialized or that Japan's silver mostly caused inflation.

Causation Explain how the fur trade affected Native American societies. 4 points

Show a model answer

At first the fur trade brought benefits, such as trade goods that strengthened chiefs' authority and alliances. But it also brought devastating disease that killed about half the Hurons, more deadly warfare including wars to capture people to replace the dead, dependence on European goods that wiped out traditional crafts, overhunting of beaver and deer, and destructive alcohol. Women's roles shifted too, as Ojibwa women lost authority even though they controlled the trade in wild rice and maple syrup.

Show how it's scored

4 points: 1 point each for up to four accurate, explained effects: early benefits (trade goods, stronger chiefs, reasonable prices); disease (for example, about half the Hurons dying); more intense and deadly warfare, mourning wars, and being drawn into French-British rivalry; dependence on European goods and loss of traditional crafts; overhunting and environmental damage; alcohol; changes in women's roles (marriages to traders, Ojibwa women losing authority). Reject: claims that Native Americans were forced laborers in the fur trade or that Europeans did most of the trapping.

Comparison Compare the North American and Siberian fur trades. Give one similarity and one difference. 4 points

Show a model answer

Similarity: in both regions, native peoples suffered from disease, became dependent on outsiders' goods, lost land to settlers, and saw fur-bearing animals seriously depleted. Difference: in North America several European nations competed and generally bought furs through negotiation, while in Siberia Russia faced no rivals and forced every able-bodied man to pay a tribute in furs, taking hostages to enforce it.

Show how it's scored

4 points: 2 for the similarity and 2 for the difference. For each, 1 point for an accurate, specific point and 1 point for showing it in BOTH regions. Similarities: disease; dependence on outsiders' goods; settler encroachment; depletion of fur-bearing animals; fur enriching outsiders and feeding global markets. Differences: competition among European powers in North America versus no rivals in Siberia; negotiated commercial trade versus forced fur tribute enforced with hostages; native trappers in North America versus many private Russian hunters competing with Siberians. Reject: claims that Siberians negotiated freely with competing powers or that North American natives paid fur tribute to a European state.

Causation Explain why Africa became the main source of enslaved labor for the plantations of the Americas. 4 points

Show a model answer

Sugar plantations needed huge amounts of brutal labor, and earlier sources dried up: the Ottoman capture of Constantinople cut off Slavic slaves, Native Americans died from disease, poor Europeans were Christians and supposedly exempt, and indentured servants were costly and temporary. Africans were skilled farmers with some immunity to tropical and European diseases, were not Christian, were relatively close, and were available in large numbers through African trade networks. Racism soon developed to justify the system.

Show how it's scored

4 points: 1 point each for up to four accurate, explained reasons: the labor demands of sugar and other plantations; the loss of Slavic slaves after the Ottoman capture of Constantinople; Native American deaths from disease; Europeans being Christian or indentured servants being expensive and temporary; Africans' farming skills and partial immunity; Africans not being Christian; proximity and existing Portuguese links to West Africa; African-run commercial networks supplying captives; papal permission; racism used to justify slavery. Reject: claims that Africans were already Christians or that Europeans conquered Africa to get slaves.

Comparison Compare the Atlantic slave system with slavery in the Islamic world. Give one similarity and one difference. 4 points

Show a model answer

Similarity: both systems enslaved Africans and treated people as property traded over long distances, drawing captives from outsiders and marginal groups. Difference: in the Islamic world most enslaved people worked in households, women were preferred, and some slaves rose to military or political power, while Atlantic slavery was far larger, centered on plantation labor that favored men, inherited across generations with little hope of freedom, and tied wholly to race and 'blackness.'

Show how it's scored

4 points: 2 for the similarity and 2 for the difference. For each, 1 point for an accurate, specific point and 1 point for showing it in BOTH systems. Similarities: enslavement of Africans; people treated as property; long-distance trade in captives; captives drawn from outsiders. Differences: domestic labor and preference for women in the Islamic world versus plantation labor and preference for men in the Atlantic system; some enslaved people rising to military or political power in the Islamic world; the huge scale of Atlantic slavery; inherited status with little hope of freedom; the racial dimension of Atlantic slavery. Reject: claims that Islamic-world slavery was mainly plantation labor or that Atlantic slavery favored women.

Change Explain how the Atlantic slave trade affected African societies. 4 points

Show a model answer

The slave trade slowed Africa's population growth and brought economic stagnation, since elites who profited did not invest in their societies. It fostered corruption, as courts were manipulated to produce captives, and it increased women's workloads and polygyny because more men were taken. Small societies were disrupted by raids, kingdoms like Kongo and Oyo broke apart, while some states like Dahomey built their power on the trade and Benin tried to limit it.

Show how it's scored

4 points: 1 point each for up to four accurate, explained effects: slower population growth; economic stagnation and lack of investment; moral and judicial corruption; heavier workloads, polygyny, and more female slaves within Africa; opportunities for a few women such as signares or royal women; insecurity and raids on small societies; the breakup of Kongo and Oyo; states like Dahomey depending on the trade; Benin restricting it. Reject: claims that the trade caused a population collapse like the Great Dying or that Europeans conquered Africa during this era.

Context Using the idea of 'agency,' explain how non-Europeans helped shape the Atlantic world. 3 points

Agency means the ability to act and affect events. Indigenous rulers joined the Spanish to defeat the Aztec and Inca empires, African rulers and merchants captured and sold people to European traders, and enslaved and colonized people resisted through the Pueblo Rebellion, runaway slave communities in Brazil, and the Haitian Revolution, while also blending Christianity with their own traditions.

Show how it's scored

Up to 2 points: specific examples of non-European agency, 1 each (indigenous allies of the conquistadores; African rulers and merchants running the supply of captives; the Pueblo Rebellion; runaway slave communities; the Haitian Revolution; adapting Christianity to their own needs). Big picture questions

These pull the whole chapter together. Outline an answer, then compare it with the suggested points.

To what extent did Europeans change older patterns of trade in Asia, and how did they adapt to them?

Changes: armed shipping, forts and passes, Dutch control of spice production, Spanish rule in the Philippines, and a shift to mass-market goods. Adaptations: Europeans joined the carrying trade, married into local societies, watched older routes revive, and depended on powerful Asian states and merchants.

How should responsibility for the transatlantic slave trade be divided among the groups involved?

European demand drove the trade, and Europeans controlled shipping and plantations; African rulers and merchants controlled capture and sale. Weigh power, profit, and choice, and note the difficulty of judging from uneven sources without blaming the victims.

What legacies of early modern globalization can you still see today?

Everyday global goods like tea, coffee, sugar, chocolate, and cotton; African diaspora cultures and racial inequality in the Americas; environmental change; and the return of Asian economies to the center of world trade.

How did Asians, Africans, and Native Americans experience European expansion differently, and how did they act as agents?

Strong Asian states like Mughal India and Japan controlled Europeans, and Asian merchants thrived. African rulers controlled the supply of captives and made different choices, as in Benin and Dahomey. Native Americans suffered disease and conquest, yet acted as traders, allies, and resisters.
